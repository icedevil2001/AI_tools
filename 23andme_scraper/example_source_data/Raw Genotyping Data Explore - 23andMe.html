Your Raw Genotyping Data

Browse your genotype data in its raw, uninterpreted format (your A's, T's, G's, and C's).* You can search for specific genes, markers, or positions of interest. Note that at this time this feature does not support data from DNA sequencing or other laboratory tests.

Genes Marker Assembly Position Variants Your Genotype
Genes Genes are segments of DNA that tell your body how to function and what traits to express. People have about 22,000 genes in their genome. Most of these come in duplicate - one copy from your mother and one from your father. Everyone has the same set of genes, but each one can vary by a few letters (bases) between people. These "variants" can lead to differences in the way you look, how you respond to stimuli, and whether or not you are predisposed to certain diseases. Marker A marker is a specific location in the genome where a genetic sequence has been shown to vary between people. Markers are denoted by a unique identifier, most often an "rs number", or "rsid". 23andMe also uses "i numbers" internally to denote certain variants. Assembly A reference human genome is assembled by scientists as a representative example of the nucleotide sequence of the human genome. It is occasionally revised, and a new version of the genome, known as a "reference assembly" or just "assembly", is released. 23andMe reports your raw results based on the Build 37 and Build 38 assemblies from the Genome Reference Consortium (GRC). Position If you stretched out all of the DNA in a chromosome from end to end, you could count the position of each letter (A,C,T,G) relative to the first one in the sequence. This count is referred to as a genome coordinate, or position. 23andMe uses the same coordinates as the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), build 37. Variants At any position in the genome that varies, there is more than one possible version (or variant) of the DNA sequence. For example, some people might have an A at a certain position, whereas other people might have a T. 23andMe always refers to the variant observed on the "plus", or forward strand of DNA (each chromosome is composed of two strands). The symbol "—" is used to denote a deletion. Your Genotype Your genotype at a marker is the combination of variants that you have at that position on both chromosomes' copies. For example, if you have the A on one chromosome copy and a T on the other one, your genotype is AT. Some chromosomes do not come in pairs (i.e. the mitochondrial chromosome and, for the most part, the X and Y chromosomes in men), so your genotype can sometimes be a single letter.

Is this answer helpful?

If your search does not produce any results it may be because you are searching for a marker or position that is not tested by 23andMe.

If you are searching for a marker by name it is possible that 23andMe uses an alternative marker name. In this case you may have better success searching for the position on the build 37 reference assembly (GRCh37). For most "rs" markers this information can be obtained from NCBI’s dbSNP.

Is this answer helpful?

A reference human genome is assembled by scientists as a representative example of the nucleotide sequence of the human genome. It is occasionally revised to account for new discoveries and fix errors, and a new version of the genome, known as a "reference assembly" (or just "assembly") is released.

Build 37 and Build 38 are reference assemblies from the Genome Reference Consortium (GRC). Build 37 was released in 2009, and Build 38 was released in 2013. Build 38 contains regions that have been updated or corrected since Build 37 was released.

Is this answer helpful?

Each chromosome consists of two strands of DNA that are complementary to each other. The DNA nucleotide base adenine (A) always pairs with thymine (T) and the base guanine (G) always pairs with cytosine (C) across these two strands. One strand is called the positive (+) strand and the other is called the negative (-) strand.

Your genotype result can differ between Build 37 and Build 38 on some markers if their locations were changed from the positive (+) strand to the negative (-) strand in Build 38. This has occurred in approximately 0.1% of markers genotyped by 23andMe.

This means that if the specified marker is one of the 0.1% affected by a strand change, your genotypes on Build 37 and Build 38 will be complementary. For example, if your genotype for the specified marker on Build 37 is “A / G”, then your genotype on Build 38 will be “T / C”.

If you are comparing your raw data results to a research paper, please be sure to verify that you are looking up your genotype for the correct assembly.

Is this answer helpful?

“N/A” means that 23andMe is unable to provide your genotype result for the specified marker on Build 38. This is the case for less than 0.1% of the markers genotyped by 23andMe.
